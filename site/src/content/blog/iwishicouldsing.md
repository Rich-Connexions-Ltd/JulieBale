In thirty-five years of teaching, I have heard these five words more times than I can count: *I wish I could sing.*

They come from all kinds of people. Business leaders. Retired teachers. Young professionals. Grandmothers. People who have spent their whole lives believing that singing was a party they simply weren't invited to.

And here's what I know: almost none of them are right.

"I wish I could sing" is almost never a statement about the voice. It's a statement about a belief. A belief formed early — usually from one critical comment, one bad experience, one careless assessment from someone who should have known better. Primary school teachers who sorted children into "singers" and "non-singers." Choir directors who quietly suggested someone mime rather than sing. Family members who laughed at the wrong moment.

These moments plant a seed. The seed grows into a belief. The belief becomes fact. And thirty, forty, fifty years later, the person is still living inside that story — still accepting it as the truth about themselves.

It is not the truth.

True tone deafness — the genuine neurological inability to distinguish pitch — affects approximately 4% of the population. The rest of us can sing. Not all of us have been taught *how* to sing. But that is a completely different problem, and it is one that can be solved.

If you find yourself saying "I wish I could sing," I invite you to question where that wish came from. And then to ask: *what if the story isn't true?*

Because in my experience, it almost certainly isn't.
