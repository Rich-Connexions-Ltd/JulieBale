The short answer is yes. The longer answer is: you already do it constantly, you're just not calling it that.

Every time you get so absorbed in a book that you don't hear someone calling your name — that's a hypnotic state. Every time you drive a familiar route and arrive without remembering the journey — that's a hypnotic state. The deeply focused, relaxed, highly receptive state that we call hypnosis is a completely natural human experience. You haven't been waiting to be introduced to it. You've been living in it, intermittently, your whole life.

What formal self-hypnosis does is teach you to enter that state deliberately, and use it for a specific purpose.

As a qualified clinical hypnotherapist who has used self-hypnosis with singers, speakers, and people managing anxiety, weight, and chronic stress, I can tell you that the results are remarkable when the practice is done correctly. Key word: correctly.

Self-hypnosis is not willpower. It is not positive thinking. It is not affirmations, though you might use those within the practice. It is a specific technique for accessing the subconscious mind — the part that holds your habits, your beliefs, and your deeply ingrained patterns — in a state of receptivity that normal waking consciousness doesn't allow.

The process is simpler than most people expect:
1. Find a quiet place and a comfortable position.
2. Use a deepening technique to move from waking consciousness into a relaxed, focused state.
3. Introduce the specific change or suggestion you want to make.
4. Close the session gently and return to full awareness.

Practiced regularly — even ten minutes a day — this changes things. Performance confidence. The relationship with food. Chronic anxiety. Limiting beliefs. The inner critic.
