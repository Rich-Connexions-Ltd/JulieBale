Something remarkable happens when a singer performs for a cause they care about. The nerves are still there. The voice still needs to work. But there is a quality to the performance that is genuinely different — a sense of purpose that changes not just how the singer sounds, but who they are in those moments.

I've witnessed this with my own students, many of whom have performed at charity concerts for causes ranging from cancer charities to mental health organisations. Every time, without exception, they report the same thing afterwards: it was the most meaningful performance of their lives.

I think I understand why. When you sing for yourself — even the most confident, accomplished singer — a part of your attention is always on you. On how you sound. On how you're coming across. The performance has you at its centre.

When you sing for something beyond yourself, that self-consciousness lifts. The voice is no longer in service of your ego. It's in service of something real — of the people in the room, of the cause, of the moment itself.

This is actually one of the most effective techniques I know for performance anxiety. If you're terrified of performing, find a reason to perform that has nothing to do with you. Give your voice a mission that matters.

The voice, it turns out, rises to meet the moment when the moment is bigger than the singer.

If you've been looking for a first performance, a charity concert might be exactly the right one.
