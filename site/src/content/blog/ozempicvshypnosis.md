Ozempic is everywhere. Its active ingredient, semaglutide, has become one of the most talked-about pharmaceutical interventions of the decade — promising significant weight loss with relatively little effort on the part of the person taking it.

I'm not going to tell you that Ozempic doesn't work. The clinical evidence suggests it does, at least for weight loss while you're taking it. But as a clinical hypnotherapist who has worked with weight management for many years, I want to raise a question that the headlines rarely address: *what happens when you stop?*

The data on this is fairly consistent. When people stop taking semaglutide, a significant proportion regain the weight. This isn't a character failing or a lack of willpower. It's the predictable consequence of treating a symptom without addressing its cause.

Because for most people, the question of weight is not primarily a metabolic or hormonal question. It is a psychological one. It is about the relationship between emotional states and eating behaviour. About the beliefs we hold about our bodies. About the patterns — often deeply ingrained since childhood — that connect stress, comfort, reward, and punishment with food.

These patterns are not addressed by reducing appetite. They are still there, waiting, when the drug is stopped.

This is where hypnotherapy approaches the problem differently. Not by suppressing hunger, but by working directly with the psychological patterns that drive the behaviour in the first place. Not a quick fix — but a genuine, lasting shift in the relationship with food.

Is hypnotherapy right for everyone? No. Are pharmaceutical interventions sometimes the appropriate tool? Absolutely. But if you're looking for a change that sticks — one that transforms not just what you weigh but how you relate to your body and to food — the mind is where the work needs to happen.
